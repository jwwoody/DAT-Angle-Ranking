How to Rank Angles on the DAT

Angle ranking, which the ADA calls angle discrimination, is one of the six parts of the Perceptual Ability Test (PAT) on the DAT. The angles are often only a few degrees apart, so it can feel like guessing at first. With practice you get noticeably better at it. Below is what the questions look like and some methods that help.

The Questions

Each question shows four angles labeled 1 through 4. You rank them from smallest to largest and pick the answer choice with that order. The angles point in different directions and the arms are different lengths, so you can't line them up and compare directly. For the current number of questions and time limit, see the DAT Candidate Guide from the American Dental Association.

Look at the Vertex

An angle's size is how far apart the two arms are at the point where they meet. Arm length doesn't change it. Long arms spread farther apart at their ends, which makes an angle look bigger. Look at the first bit of each arm next to the vertex and ignore the ends of the lines.

Ways to Compare Close Angles

Find the smallest or largest first

Usually one angle is clearly the smallest or the largest. Once you know one end of the order, you only have to sort the remaining two or three.

Use the answer choices

You don't have to rank all four from scratch. If angle 3 is clearly the smallest, cross out every choice that doesn't start with 3. Often you only need one more comparison after that.

Compare each angle to 90 degrees

Right angles are easy to spot. Ask whether each angle is a bit under 90, about 90, or a bit over. For smaller angles, use 45 degrees (half a right angle) instead. Comparing each angle to the same reference is easier than comparing four angles to each other.

Turn the angle in your head

Picture each angle rotated so one arm lies flat, then look at how high the other arm goes. Rotating them all the same way makes them easier to compare.

Compare two at a time

If two angles look the same, ignore the other two and look back and forth between just those two. It's easier to see a small difference in a pair than across all four.

Don't spend too long on one

If you have a first impression, go with it. If you're stuck, pick your best guess, mark the question, and come back to it if you have time.

Common Mistakes

  • Going by line length. Longer arms make an angle look bigger. Check the vertex.
  • Being thrown off by direction. An angle opening upward can look different from the same angle opening to the side.
  • Ranking largest to smallest. Read the question and rank smallest to largest.
  • Running out of time. Spending a long time on one angle question takes time away from the rest of the PAT.

A Practice Plan

Here is one way to use the practice tool:

  1. Start easy. Use a large angle difference, like 20 or 30 degrees, and use the methods above on every problem, even the easy ones.
  2. Lower the difference slowly. When you're getting almost every problem right, drop the difference by a few degrees. If you start missing a lot, go back up for a while.
  3. Practice a little most days. Ten or fifteen minutes a day works better than one long session a week.
  4. Look at what you missed. When you get one wrong, check the correct order and figure out why. Was it line length, direction, or rushing?
  5. Speed up later. Once you're accurate at small differences, work faster to get closer to test pace.

Angle ranking is one part of the PAT. Use official ADA materials and full practice tests for the rest of the DAT.
